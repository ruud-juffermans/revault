More and more often, relatives clearing out a home find something that points to crypto: a hardware wallet, a laptop with Electrum or Bitcoin Core, a printout with a long string of characters, a note with twelve or twenty-four English words. You don't know what it is worth, whether it is worth anything, or whom to trust. This is a sober sequence of steps.

## 1. Throw nothing away, reset nothing, try nothing

The most important step, and the only one that is urgent. Don't discard devices, even if they seem broken. Don't reset a phone or hardware wallet "to see if it still works" — a reset erases the keys for good. Don't enter a PIN on a Ledger or Trezor: after three wrong attempts the device wipes itself. Keep notes exactly as you found them, crossed-out words included.

## 2. Take inventory

Make a list: which devices, which programs on them, which papers and files. Photos are fine; don't type anything into an email or chat. Check the deceased's email for confirmations from exchanges (Coinbase, Kraken, Binance, Bitvavo) — that is a separate route: an exchange account is not a wallet, and those balances are claimed directly from the exchange with a certificate of inheritance.

## 3. Check whether there is anything

With a public address (from a program, an email or a transaction confirmation) you can see on a blockchain explorer whether there is a balance, without having access to anything. This often already answers whether further steps are worth it. A Ledger with an empty address is not a case; a wallet.dat with a ten-year-old balance is.

## 4. Involve the notary or executor early

Crypto is part of the estate and has to be declared. More important for you: a recovery with me never starts without verification of the inheritance position. That is not a formality; it is what allows me to do the work at all. A certificate of inheritance, a will or an appointment as executor makes that verification straightforward. Notaries and executors are welcome to contact me directly; I work at their office if preferred.

## 5. Establish what is missing

In an estate it is usually the password or PIN that is missing, not the wallet itself. That is recoverable in many cases: based on the deceased's password habits, old password managers, notes and the device itself. A seed phrase with a few words missing or illegible can often be reconstructed. A seed phrase that doesn't exist at all, and a device that has been reset, cannot — and I'd rather say so immediately than after weeks.

## What happens next

After an [intake](/en/contact) I assess, based on your inventory, whether a recovery is promising. If so, an agreement with the heirs or the executor follows, then verification, and only then the work. The crypto is never transferred to me: I restore access, you or the notary handle the transfer. Payment is a percentage of the recovered value and is only due after a successful recovery.

Not sure whether something is crypto-related? Include a photo of the device or printout with your question — without words or keys — and you'll hear quickly whether it is worth pursuing.
